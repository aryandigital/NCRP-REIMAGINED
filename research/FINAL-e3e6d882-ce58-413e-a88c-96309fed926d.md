# Visual Perception & UX Psychology for a High-Stakes Cybercrime Victim Reporting Portal

**Scope note on the user.** Every principle below is filtered through one fact: this user has, within hours or days, been deliberately manipulated by a skilled adversary who used urgency, authority, and scarcity against them. They arrive with (a) acutely elevated arousal, (b) degraded working memory, (c) active shame, and (d) a freshly-calibrated, hair-trigger suspicion of any interface that pressures them. Design decisions that would be merely suboptimal on a normal site are actively harmful here.

Two research findings should anchor the whole design:

1. **Mental noise reduces information-processing capacity by up to 80%** in high-concern situations (Covello's Mental Noise Theory, adopted into CDC's CERC doctrine). Under stress, people can process roughly **three messages, not seven**, and they systematically miss the middle of any list. â€” [NOAA Practical Guide, p. on mental noise](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-08/Natural_Hazard_Risk_Communication_Practical_Guide.pdf), [Covello & Milligan, NRC ML101590283](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1015/ML101590283.pdf)
2. **Panic is rare; the condition most conducive to panic is conflicting messages from authority.** CDC CERC: "People are most likely to panic when they feel that they can't trust what those in authority are telling them or when they feel misled or abandoned in dangerous territory." â€” [CERC Quick Guide](https://dhhr.wv.gov/healthprep/about/archives/Documents/CERC%20Quick%20Guide%202008.pdf)

The design implication of #2 is structural: **inconsistency between what the page promises and what the service delivers is the single largest panic risk**, larger than any color or typography choice.

## 1. Visual Hierarchy and Eye-Guidance

### 1.1 Preattentive processing

**Mechanism.** A set of low-level visual features is extracted in parallel across the entire visual field, before conscious attention is deployed. The operational threshold is **200â€“250 ms**, chosen because a saccade takes at least ~200 ms to initiate â€” so anything detected inside that window was detected *without* moving the eyes. â€” [Healey, "Perception in Visualization," NC State](https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/healey/PP/)

The established preattentive attributes: **hue, luminance/intensity, size, length, width, orientation, curvature, shape, enclosure/added marks, spatial position (2D), density, depth cues, direction of motion, flicker.** â€” [Healey & Enns via Huppenkothen](https://huppenkothen.org/data-visualization-tutorial/04-preattentiveprocessing/index.html), [Healey & Enns 1993, Graphics Interface](https://graphicsinterface.org/wp-content/uploads/gi1993-14.pdf)

**The critical constraint â€” Treisman's Feature Integration Theory.** A target defined by *one* feature pops out in constant time regardless of how many distractors are present. A target defined by a **conjunction of two features** (e.g., "the red circle among red squares and blue circles") requires **serial search**, and search time scales linearly with the number of items. â€” [datafield.dev Ch.2](https://datafield.dev/data-visualization-python/part-01/chapter-02/)

**Feature interference is asymmetric** (Callaghan). Varying hue interferes with form/shape segregation, but varying form does not interfere with hue segregation. Varying brightness interferes with hue segregation, but hue does not interfere with brightness. Practical ordering of channel strength: **luminance > hue > size > shape/curvature.** â€” [Healey & Enns 1993](https://graphicsinterface.org/wp-content/uploads/gi1993-14.pdf)

**Design decisions:**

- **One preattentive attribute per view, spent on one element.** Pick the channel and reserve it. For a crisis portal I recommend **saturated hue as the scarce currency**: exactly one saturated-color element per viewport â€” the primary action. Everything else is greyscale, near-black text, and one desaturated neutral. If your page has a colored badge, a colored link, a colored icon, *and* a colored button, you have spent the currency four times and bought nothing.
- **Do not encode the primary action with a conjunction.** "The large blue button" fails if there are other large elements and other blue elements. Make it the *only* filled, saturated element â€” a single-feature target.
- **Concrete spec for the focal CTA:** filled solid brand color at â‰¥3:1 against page background (WCAG 1.4.11), label text â‰¥4.5:1 against the fill, minimum height 56px on mobile / 48px desktop, and **no other filled saturated element above the fold**. Secondary actions become text links or 1px-outline ghost buttons with a neutral border, which sit in a *different* channel (enclosure without fill) and therefore do not compete.
- **Because luminance masks hue,** never rely on a hue difference alone to distinguish two adjacent important elements if their luminance is similar. Give the focal element a luminance step of at least ~30 L\* from its neighbors, not just a hue step.
- **Motion is the strongest attribute and is therefore banned** on this site except for (a) focus/state feedback under 200ms and (b) determinate progress. See Â§7.

### 1.2 Von Restorff / isolation effect

**Mechanism.** Hedwig von Restorff (1933): an item that differs in kind from a homogeneous series is recalled far better, *regardless of its serial position*. Distinctiveness overrides position. â€” [Yu-kai Chou, Von Restorff](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/von-restorff-isolation-effect-distinctiveness-memory/)

The effect is **contextual, not absolute**: the oddball is memorable for being *the only* odd thing. Adding a second oddball roughly halves the effect; adding six destroys it.

**Design decisions:**

- Use isolation to rescue the **one thing users must not miss but which cannot structurally go first or last** â€” typically the "preserve your evidence, do not delete the messages" instruction, which naturally falls in the middle of a preparation list. Give it a distinct treatment (e.g., left-border callout, different background, icon) that appears **exactly once on the page**.
- Audit rule, run on every screen: *count the elements competing for attention. If the count is >1, the design has not yet decided what matters.*
- Corollary: if your design system has "info," "warning," "success," and "tip" callouts, and a page uses three of them, none of them isolates. Cap decorated callouts at **one per viewport-height of scroll**.

### 1.3 Scanning patterns (NN/g eyetracking)

NN/g identifies four text-scanning patterns, ordered worstâ†’best for comprehension. â€” [NN/g, Text Scanning Patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/), [NN/g, F-Shaped Pattern Revisited](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)

| Pattern | What happens | When it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **F-pattern** | Two horizontal sweeps at the top, then a vertical scan down the left edge; right-side content is skipped | Unformatted walls of text with no headings, bullets, or bold |
| **Spotted** | Eyes jump to visually distinct tokens â€” links, bold words, digits, addresses â€” skipping everything between | User hunting for a specific fact (a phone number, a deadline, a reference ID) |
| **Layer-cake** | Eyes traverse headings and subheadings only, dropping into body text only when a heading matches the goal | Well-structured content with descriptive subheadings â€” **the most efficient pattern** |
| **Commitment** | Near-total fixation on all content words | Rare; only when highly motivated *and* the source is trusted |

NN/g is explicit that **the F-pattern is a failure state**: "the F-shaped scanning pattern is bad for users and businesses... Good web formatting reduces the impact of F-scanning."

**Which applies here.** A crisis reporting portal has **two distinct page types and must design for two different patterns:**

- **The task pages (report form, triage, "what to do right now")** are *not* content pages. Users are goal-directed and time-pressured, and they will exhibit the **spotted pattern**, hunting for the one action that matches their situation. Design for spotted: make the scan-anchors (action verbs, amounts, deadlines, phone numbers) the bolded/distinct tokens, and make sure that a user who reads *nothing but the bold tokens* still gets a correct instruction sequence. Never bold a word that is not itself an anchor.
- **The guidance/explainer pages** ("what happens after you report," "how to recover your accounts") must be engineered for **layer-cake**. That means: a descriptive subheading every **3â€“5 short paragraphs** (roughly every 150â€“200 words), subheadings written as the *answer* not the topic ("You will get a reference number within 24 hours," not "Next steps"), and the constraint that **reading only the subheadings must yield a complete, correct summary.** That is the single highest-leverage content rule on the site.
- **Never rely on commitment.** Even though motivation is extremely high here (Fogg's #1 prominence factor), acute stress suppresses the sustained attention commitment requires. Assume scanning.

### 1.4 Gutenberg diagram, Z-pattern, and where they actually apply

**Gutenberg diagram** divides a display into four quadrants: **primary optical area (top-left)**, **strong fallow (top-right)**, **weak fallow (bottom-left)**, **terminal area (bottom-right)**. The eye enters at the primary optical area and moves in successive left-to-right sweeps ("axes of orientation") along "reading gravity" toward the terminal area. The fallow areas receive minimal attention *unless visually emphasized*. â€” [Universal Principles of Design, 3rd ed., Â§086](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/universal-principles-of/9780760375174/xhtml/ch86.xhtml), [Vanseo Design](https://vanseodesign.com/web-design/3-design-layouts/)

The crucial caveat, stated by nearly every serious source: **Gutenberg describes eye movement through *evenly distributed, homogeneous* information.** It is a description of what happens when you have failed to build a hierarchy â€” a default, not a target. "Instead of trying to force your design into one of the patterns described, decide what information you want the viewer to see and through a series of focal points... lead their eyes through your hierarchy."

**Design decisions:**

- **Do not design a Z-pattern hero for this site.** The Z-pattern is a conversion-page device for sparse, visual-heavy marketing layouts with one commercial goal. It reads as marketing. It also strands the primary action in the bottom-right, which is wrong for a task the user must start immediately.
- **Use a single-column layout** (max ~680â€“720px measure, see Â§5.1) so that "reading gravity" collapses to a straight vertical line and there are no fallow areas to strand content in. Single-column is the correct crisis layout because it removes the *decision of where to look*, which is itself extraneous load.
- **Exploit the primary optical area literally:** the top-left of the content column carries the page's one-sentence orienting statement (what this page is, what it will do for you). Do not put a logo lockup, a hero image, or a search box there.
- **The terminal position (bottom of the single column) gets the recency slot** â€” see Â§4.4. This is where the "you are safe to stop here / here is what happens next" close goes.
- If you must emphasize something in a structurally weak position, that is exactly the von Restorff use case (Â§1.2).

### 1.5 Gestalt principles with numeric spacing

- **Figure/ground** â€” the focal element must be unambiguously figure. Never place text over photography on this site. Give the content column a distinct surface (e.g., white card on a very light neutral, or simply white on white with generous margins) so the reading area is unambiguous.

**Responsive rule:** preserve grouping *intent*, not numeric ratios. When collapsing to mobile, section gaps may shrink from 64px â†’ 40px, but the internal â‰¤50% relationship must survive. Never uniformly scale all spacing by one factor.

## 2. Cognitive Load and Decision-Making

### 2.1 Cognitive Load Theory

Sweller's three loads share **one fixed working-memory budget**; intrinsic and extraneous are additive. â€” [Paas & van MerriÃ«nboer 2020, *Current Directions in Psych Science*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721420922183)

- **Intrinsic** â€” inherent complexity/element interactivity of the material. On this site: the report itself is genuinely complex (dates, amounts, transaction references, platform names, what the scammer said). You cannot delete this load; you can only **sequence** it.
- **Extraneous** â€” load created purely by presentation. This is entirely the designer's fault and can be driven to near zero. It is the cheapest, highest-return work.
- **Germane** â€” productive processing. Protect the capacity freed by cutting extraneous load.

**The two named effects that matter most in UI:**

**Split-attention effect** â€” learners perform worse when interdependent information is separated in space or time, because they must mentally integrate it. Integrate it physically instead. â€” [Ayres & Sweller, *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, ch.15](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/splitattention-principle-in-multimedia-learning/194CBCD1A3C911116CCB5F403AC7E415)

> **Design decisions:** Help text goes **immediately below its field**, never in a sidebar, tooltip, accordion, or separate "Help" page. Example/format hints go *inside or directly under* the input ("For example, 12 3 2026"). Error messages go **adjacent to the offending field**, not only in a summary at the top (do both â€” summary for screen readers and orientation, inline for repair). "What counts as evidence?" belongs inline at the evidence-upload step, not on a linked page. Every link that says "learn more" and navigates away is a split-attention event that costs a working-memory reload.

**Redundancy effect** â€” adding a second copy of information the user already has *hurts*. Narration identical to on-screen text, a paragraph re-explaining a self-explanatory diagram, "helpful" captions on an obvious visual: each forces reconciliation of two streams. "The kindest thing you can do with redundant material is delete it." â€” [CLT overview](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/cognitive-load-theory-sweller-intrinsic-extraneous-germane/)

> **Design decision:** delete the introductory paragraph that restates the H1. Delete the icon that duplicates the label with no added meaning. If a step is titled "Tell us what happened," do not follow it with "In this step, we'll ask you to tell us what happened."

**The extraneous-load audit, in priority order:**
1. Split attention (help/label/error separated from field)
2. Redundancy (duplicate explanations, decorative icons with no added meaning)
3. Inconsistent interaction patterns (two different date pickers; "Continue" vs "Next" vs "Save and continue" on different steps)
4. Decorative animation and seductive detail (illustrations, hero imagery, stock photos of hooded figures at keyboards â€” these add load *and* add fear)
5. Navigation that requires holding position in a hierarchy in mind

### 2.2 Miller's Law and the modern critique

Miller (1956) measured **short-term recall** â€” holding items in mind with nothing to look at. Later work (Cowan 2001) puts the true capacity closer to **~4 chunks**, and highly variable.

Applying "7Â±2" to a visible menu or a list of options is a **category error**: a visible menu is a *recognition* task, not recall. Nothing enters the working-memory bottleneck. "A restaurant menu can list forty dishes. A settings page can show thirty rows." â€” [StÃ©phanie Walter](https://stephaniewalter.design/blog/your-menu-doesnt-need-millers-7-plus-minus-2-rule/), [Miller's Law critique](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/millers-magical-number-seven-plus-minus-two-working-memory/)

**What survives from Miller is chunking**, which is genuinely the useful contribution.

**Design decisions:**
- **Do not cap visible option lists at 7.** If there are 14 legitimate crime types, show all 14 â€” grouped and labeled. Amputating the list to hit a number forces users into an "Other" bucket, which degrades triage quality and makes the victim feel unseen.
- **Do enforce the ~4-chunk limit on anything the user must carry in their head across a screen boundary.** Under stress, plan for 3â€“4, not 7 â€” and CERC's crisis-specific guidance is **three**.
- **Never require carrying a value forward.** If a reference number is generated on step 3 and needed on step 6, display it persistently in a sticky element, prefill it, or both. Never show a code and then navigate away from it.
- **Chunk all long strings for the eye:** reference numbers as `NCR-2026-8412-77`, amounts with thousands separators, phone numbers in national grouping, dates spelled out (`24 August 2026`, not `24/08/26` â€” ambiguous internationally and harder to verify under stress).

### 2.3 Hick's Law and its limits

Hickâ€“Hyman: choice reaction time grows logarithmically with the number of equiprobable alternatives. But the CHI 2020 review by Liu et al. is blunt about its misuse:

> "(1) Hick's law speaks **against**, not for, the popular principle that 'less is better'; (2) logarithmic growth of observed temporal data is not necessarily interpretable in terms of Hick's law; (3) the stimulus-response paradigm is rarely relevant to HCI tasks... (4) a detailed examination of visual search and decision making is more fruitful than a mere reference to Hick's law."
> â€” [Liu, Rioul, Beaudouin-Lafon, Guiard, CHI 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376878)

The law describes **overlearned, high stimulusâ€“response-compatibility reaction time**. For an unfamiliar interface the dominant cost is **linear visual search**, not logarithmic decision. And the logarithm is precisely why *adding* options is cheap: going from 8 to 16 options costs one extra bit, not double the time.

**Design decisions:**
- **When more choices is fine:** when options are (a) mutually exclusive, (b) easily distinguishable by label, (c) grouped under meaningful headers, and (d) the user has a clear pre-existing preference. A list of 20 well-grouped scam types is fine. Users know whether they were scammed on a dating app or by a fake bank caller.
- **The fix for many options is grouping, not deletion.** A labeled category turns one flat search into a two-step hierarchy: one coarse decision (which group?) then one fine decision (which item?). Group the crime-type list into 4â€“6 categories of 3â€“6 items each.
- **When fewer choices genuinely helps:** at the *single decision point that gates everything else*. - Hick's real, defensible design contribution: **maximize stimulusâ€“response compatibility.** The button that starts a report should say "Start my report," be at the top, and be the only filled button. That is the S-R compatibility win, and it dwarfs any argument about counting options.

### 2.4 Choice overload and progressive disclosure

The evidence here is genuinely contested and you should know that:

- **Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010)**: meta-analysis of 63 conditions, 50 experiments, N=5,036 â†’ **mean effect size virtually zero**, but with substantial between-study variance and no identifiable *sufficient* conditions. â€” [JCR 37(3)](https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf)
- **Chernev, BÃ¶ckenholt & Goodman (2015)**: 99 observations, N=7,202 â†’ the effect *is* significant once you account for four moderators: **choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal.** â€” [J. Consumer Psychology](https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002)

**Why this matters here.** Chernev's four moderators are *all maximally present* for a distressed fraud victim: the task is difficult (time pressure, unfamiliar vocabulary), the set is complex (crime categories are hard to compare and overlap), preference uncertainty is extreme (the victim genuinely does not know which category they fall into), and the goal is effort-minimizing (they want this over with). **This is the population for whom choice overload is most likely to be real.** So the honest framing is: don't cite "the paradox of choice" as universal law; cite the moderators, and note that this context hits all four.

**Progressive disclosure as the resolution:**
- **Never show more than one decision per screen** in the report flow. One question, one screen (the GOV.UK "one thing per page" pattern), with the answer determining the next question.
- **Branch, don't enumerate.** Instead of a 20-item crime-type list, ask "How did they first contact you?" (5 options) â†’ "What did they get?" (4 options) â†’ derive the category. Two 5-option decisions are far cheaper than one 20-option decision *and* they lower preference uncertainty because each question is concrete and answerable from episodic memory.
- **Reveal advanced/optional fields behind an explicit, labeled control** â€” "Add another transaction," "I have more details about the account they used" â€” never behind hover, never auto-expanding.
- **Offer an explicit "I don't know" / "I'm not sure" option on every question** and make it non-punitive in tone. Preference uncertainty is the moderator you can most directly defuse. Under stress, forcing a guess produces bad data *and* increases distress.

### 2.5 Decision fatigue

Each successive decision degrades the quality of subsequent ones, biasing toward the default, the status quo, or abandonment.

**Design decisions:**
- **Count the decisions in your flow and cut the count, not the fields.** A 20-field form where 15 fields are prefilled or single-obvious-answer is far cheaper than a 10-field form of genuine judgment calls.
- **Order decisions easyâ†’hard.** Contact details and dates first (low-judgment, builds momentum, produces endowed progress â€” Â§4.2); free-text narrative and evidence upload later.
- **Set safe defaults for every non-consequential choice** â€” but never default a consent, a data-sharing permission, or anything the user would be harmed by not noticing. Defaults on consequential choices are a dark pattern.
- **Save progress automatically and say so** ("Saved â€” you can close this and come back with your reference number"). This converts "finish now or lose everything" into a resumable task, which is the single most effective decision-fatigue mitigation available.
- **Never present a decision the user cannot yet make.** Asking "Do you want to report this to the police as well?" before explaining what that means creates a decision with no information, which is maximally fatiguing.

### 2.6 Cognitive tunneling, attentional narrowing, and Yerkes-Dodson

**Working memory is the specific casualty.** Otto et al. Construction-industry mixed-reality study: excessive stress led subjects to **miss surrounding hazards**, impairing risk perception and increasing risk-taking. â€” [NSF PAR 10684890](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10684890)

**Design decisions â€” this is the most consequential subsection in the brief:**

1. **Assume peripheral UI does not exist.** Anything in a sidebar, a footer, a top nav, a right rail, or a sticky banner is functionally invisible to a tunneled user. Every piece of information that is *required* must be in the central column, in the reading path, at the point of need. Single-column layout is not an aesthetic choice here; it is a countermeasure to attentional narrowing.
2. **Use only overlearned patterns.** No novel interactions, no custom widgets, no gesture-based controls, no carousels, no drag-to-reorder. A stressed user runs on habit: native form controls, standard buttons, standard links, browser-native scroll. Every custom component is a bet that a panicking person will learn something new.
3. **Aim for the *middle* of the Yerkes-Dodson curve, deliberately.** The user arrives over-aroused. Your job is to *reduce* arousal into the productive band, not to add to it. This is the direct justification for banning countdown timers, alarm-red full-bleed banners, and urgency copy (Â§3.6) â€” they push an already-over-aroused user further right on the curve, where performance collapses.
4. **Design for "one thing at a time" literally.** One question per screen. One action per screen. The single-question pattern is not just a progressive-disclosure device; it is the only layout that survives severe tunneling, because the tunnel contains exactly the thing you need them to see.
5. **Never require simultaneous dual-task coordination.** Do not ask users to open their banking app in another tab *while* filling a field that times out. Provide a "pause and come back" path with a resumption code.
6. **Assume errors and make them cheap.** Degraded WM produces transposition errors, wrong dates, wrong amounts. Every field must be editable at review; the review step must show all answers on one page with per-field "Change" links; nothing should be irreversible before final submission.
7. **Design the "money is still moving" fast path as a separate, single-screen artifact.** If the fraud is in progress, the user has minutes and near-zero cognitive capacity. That screen should be: one heading, one phone number at â‰¥32px, and three numbered steps of â‰¤12 words each. Nothing else. No nav, no footer links, no form.
8. **Do not ask the user to assess risk.** Impaired risk perception is a documented consequence of acute stress. Never present "Is this urgent? Yes/No" â€” instead ask observable facts ("Did you send money in the last 24 hours?" / "Do they still have access to your email?") and let the system classify.

## 3. Trust, Credibility, and Persuasion (Ethical)

### 3.1 Stanford Web Credibility research (Fogg)

The 10 guidelines, derived from three years of research with 4,500+ participants: â€” [Stanford Web Credibility Project](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines/)

**The P-I Theory implication for this audience:** user involvement is *maximal* (this is their money and safety), which means **they will notice far more than a typical visitor** â€” including small inconsistencies. ### 3.2 Aesthetic-usability effect

Kurosu & Kashimura (CHI '95): 26 ATM layout variants rated by **252 participants** on aesthetic appeal and apparent ease of use. Correlation between beauty and *apparent* usability: **0.589** â€” stronger than the correlation between beauty and *inherent* usability. â€” [Kurosu & Kashimura 1995](http://masui.org.s3.amazonaws.com/b/d/bdd052e6233fbeb0380491c7af408502.pdf), [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)

The security-relevant extension: **"What is Beautiful is Secure"** (ACM TOCHI 2022) â€” across a lab study and an online study, participants **consistently rated visually appealing websites as more secure and said they would be more likely to enter sensitive information into them, even when those sites were objectively less secure.** â€” [ACM 10.1145/3533047](https://doi.org/10.1145/3533047)

**This finding cuts both ways and you must design for both directions:**
- **Upside:** a polished portal will be trusted, and trust is a prerequisite for disclosure. Victims who do not trust the site will under-report or abandon.
- **Downside â€” and this is an ethical obligation for a fraud portal:** the same effect is *exactly the mechanism scammers exploit.* A beautiful phishing clone will out-trust an ugly real site. Therefore the portal must teach a verification heuristic that is **not** aesthetic: a persistent, plainly-worded strip near the top â€” "Always check the address bar reads `[exact-domain]`. We will never ask for your password or a one-time code." That single sentence transfers the trust judgment from an unreliable channel (beauty) to a reliable one (domain), and it is the most useful anti-fraud content on the page.

### 3.3 Halo effect and authority signals

The aesthetic-usability effect is a specific case of the **halo effect** â€” a positive impression in one dimension bleeds into unrelated dimensions. In a government context, visual polish is read as institutional competence.

E-government research supports this: a meta-analytic SEM across **68 empirical studies** found trust in government (Î²=0.123), trust in the internet (Î²=0.450), and perceived privacy/security (Î²=0.301) all significantly positive predictors of e-government trust, with perceived risk negative (Î²=âˆ’0.105). â€” [Information Systems Frontiers 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-024-10497-8)

A heuristic evaluation study found "a close correlation between usability and credibility â€” e-government websites with high usability were perceived as having higher credibility, and vice versa," and identified the usual failure modes: hard-to-understand content, inconsistent formats, poor navigation, broken links, overloaded information presentation, inconsistent colors. â€” [Government Information Quarterly](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0740624X1400121X)

**Design decisions:**
- **Reducing density is a trust intervention, not just a usability one.** This is the strongest single argument for the sparse, single-column, one-question-per-page design.
- **Authority signals to use:** official crest/seal at consistent size, full legal department name, the domain-verification strip, statute reference, "Last reviewed" dates, and the physical address.
- **Authority signals to avoid:** security badge images (McAfee/Norton-style seals), padlock graphics in the content area (these are the *exact* iconography phishing sites overuse), and "100% Secure" claims. Real security lives in the browser chrome and in HSTS, not in a GIF.
- **Beware negative halo from a single visible defect.** Because these users are hyper-vigilant, one typo, one mixed-content warning, or one certificate error will be read not as sloppiness but as *evidence of a fake*. Treat spelling and cert hygiene as security controls.

### 3.4 Signaling trustworthiness without dark patterns

The positive playbook, drawn from Fogg + CERC + trauma-informed practice:

- **Show your work (operational transparency).** State what happens to the report, who reads it, in what timeframe, and what the realistic outcomes are. See Â§4.6 â€” this is the labor illusion used honestly.
- **Honestly label limitations.** CERC's first-message structure explicitly includes **"what you don't know about the situation"** and **"what the process is"** as required elements. â€” [CERC by Leaders for Leaders](https://tsba.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Crisis-and-Emergency-Risk-Communication-By-Leaders-for-Leaders.pdf)
  > Concretely: *"Most reports do not lead to an individual prosecution. Reporting still matters, because it lets us link cases and freeze accounts â€” here's what it does do."* This is the highest-trust sentence you can write, and every instinct in a comms department will fight it. CERC's position is that uncertainty is worse than bad news and that withholding to avoid embarrassment is the fastest route to lost credibility.
- **Never overpromise recovery.** "We may be able to help recover funds if you act within X hours" is defensible if true and evidenced. "Get your money back" is not.
- **Set expectations numerically and then meet them.** "You'll get a reference number immediately and an update within 5 working days." Then send it. Consistency between promise and delivery is the anti-panic mechanism from CERC.
- **Lead with empathy, before facts.** CERC's Trust Determination Theory: "They want to know that you care before they care what you know." The first line of the confirmation page should acknowledge the person, not the case number.
- **Explicitly de-blame.** Fraud-victimization research finds internalized self-blame is directly linked to reluctance to disclose, with fear of judgment and stigma delaying help-seeking.   > Concretely: near the top of the entry page, in body-text size (not a decorated callout, which reads as boilerplate): *"These scams are designed by organised groups to work on anyone. Reporting helps."* Avoid any copy that implies the user should have known better â€” including well-meaning "How to spot a scam" content placed *before* the report path. Put prevention content *after* the report is filed.
- **Never require a user to describe why they fell for it.** Ask what happened, not why they believed it.

### 3.5 What reads as "scammy" â€” the ban list

### 3.6 Legitimate urgency vs. scam urgency

Sometimes urgency is real: some interbank recall and account-freeze mechanisms are genuinely time-sensitive. Here is how to express it so it does not read as manipulation.

| Dimension | Scam urgency | Legitimate urgency |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of the deadline** | Manufactured by the interface; resets or vanishes | External, verifiable fact (bank cut-off, statutory window) |
| **Who benefits** | The site | The user, stated explicitly |
| **Specificity** | Vague ("limited time," "act now") | Named mechanism, named actor, named window: "Banks can sometimes recall a transfer within 24 hours of sending." |
| **Countability** | Live ticking clock | Static, absolute statement of the window; **no counter** |
| **Consequence of missing it** | Threatened loss, punishment | Honestly stated reduction in options, plus a still-useful alternative: "If it's been longer, reporting still helps â€” here's why." |
| **Tone** | Imperative + exclamation | Declarative, second person, calm |
| **Reversibility** | One-way door | The user can always come back; nothing is lost by taking a minute |
| **Explanation** | None offered | The *why* is given, so the user can verify it independently |

**The operational test:** *Would this element still be here if the deadline did not exist?* If yes, it's decoration and it's manipulative. *Can the user verify the deadline from a third party?* If no, don't state it as a deadline.

**Concrete pattern for the one place real urgency exists:**

> **If you sent money in the last 24 hours, call your bank now**
> **[0000 000 0000]** â€” open 24 hours
> Banks can sometimes stop or recall a payment if you call quickly. Reporting to us does not contact your bank.
> *If it has been longer than 24 hours, still report it â€” we use reports to trace accounts and to link cases.*

Note what it does: names the actor (your bank), names the mechanism (recall), gives the number at large size, honestly discloses a limitation ("reporting to us does not contact your bank" â€” which prevents a dangerous false assumption), and provides a non-punitive path for people outside the window. No timer, no red, no exclamation mark.

## 4. Memory, Progress, and Completion Psychology

### 4.1 Zeigarnik effect and progress indicators

Zeigarnik (1927): interrupted tasks are recalled more readily than completed ones; an unfinished task maintains cognitive tension until discharged. Structurally, this is what makes a half-filled progress bar a *retention* mechanism between sessions, not merely an in-session conversion device â€” "the gradient can only operate on a goal that is cognitively live." â€” [Product Philosophy](https://productphilosophy.com/articles/goal-gradient-progress-mechanics)

**Design decisions:**
- **Show a persistent step indicator on every screen of the report flow**: "Step 3 of 6 â€” About the payment." Text label plus a bar; never a bar alone (the label is the part that survives greyscale and screen readers).
- **Design for interruption as the normal case, not the edge case.** These users will be interrupted â€” by a bank call, a partner, a police officer, or by crying. Autosave on every field blur, show "Saved" state, and issue a **resumption link/code within the first two steps** so the tension has somewhere to go other than "start over."
- **Ethical boundary:** use Zeigarnik to help the user finish a task *they want to finish*. Do not use it to create tension about optional data ("Your profile is 60% complete!"). On a victim service, a nagging incompleteness meter about optional fields is cruel.

### 4.2 Goal-gradient and endowed progress

**Goal-gradient (Hull 1932; Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006):** effort accelerates as the proportion of remaining distance shrinks. CafÃ© field experiment: customers bought coffee more frequently the closer they got to the free coffee. â€” [Kivetz et al., *JMR* 43(1)](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)

**Endowed progress (Nunes & DrÃ¨ze):** a 12-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled (requiring 10 purchases) outperforms a plain 10-stamp card â€” reported completion **~34% vs ~19%**, roughly a doubling, on an identical amount of real work. The mechanism is reframing the task from *not yet begun* to *undertaken and incomplete*. â€” [Nunes & DrÃ¨ze](https://doi.org/10.1037/e621442012-134), [Product Philosophy analysis](https://productphilosophy.com/articles/goal-gradient-progress-mechanics)

**Design decisions:**
- **Endow progress honestly.** Do not fake a step. Instead, make the first step *genuinely already done* by the time the progress bar first appears: if the user reached the form by clicking "I was scammed on a dating app," record that as completed step 1 and show the bar at 1/6. This is true, and it is the entire endowment effect.
- **Prefill everything you legitimately can** â€” date defaults to today, country from locale, contact details from an authenticated session â€” and mark those steps complete.
- **Front-load the cheap steps** so the gradient is steep early. Two 20-second steps before the hard narrative step means the user hits "3 of 6" within a minute.
- **Show proportion, not just count.** "Step 3 of 6" plus a filled bar communicates remaining distance, which is the variable the goal-gradient model actually operates on ("effort investment is a function of the proportion of original distance remaining").
- **Never reset the bar.** The documented failure mode of goal-gradient systems is the post-reward reset. If a user must supply more information later, present it as a *new, separately-named* task, not as the old bar dropping back to zero.
- **Do not inflate the step count to make each step feel small.** Users notice, and it reads as manipulation.

### 4.3 Peak-end rule

Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993): the cold-pressor study â€” 60s at 14Â°C vs. 60s at 14Â°C **plus** 30 extra seconds warming to 15Â°C. Participants preferred to repeat the *longer, objectively worse* trial, because the ending was better. Retrospective evaluation is predicted almost entirely by **peak intensity** and **final intensity**; duration is neglected. â€” [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/peak-end-rule/), [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/peak-end-rule/)

NN/g adds the critical asymmetry: **negative moments are recalled more vividly**, and moments of confusion and frustration function as peaks.

**Design decisions for this service journey:**
- **Map the journey and identify the negative peak.** For a fraud report it is almost always one of: (a) the free-text "describe what happened" field, which forces re-narration of a humiliating experience, or (b) a validation error that wipes entered data, or (c) the moment the user learns their money is probably gone.
- **Blunt the negative peak deliberately.** For the narrative field: offer structured prompts instead of a blank box ("What did they say they were? What did they ask you to do? What did you send?"), allow "I'd rather not describe this," and never impose a minimum character count. For (c): pair the bad news with the concrete thing that *is* being done, per CERC's negative-dominance guidance (Â§5.3).
- **Engineer the end as a deliberate artifact, not a system-generated receipt.** The confirmation screen is the single highest-leverage screen on the site. It should contain, in this order: (1) a plain acknowledgment that reporting was the right thing and it is now done; (2) the reference number, large and copyable, with "we've emailed this to you"; (3) exactly what happens next, with a date; (4) the **three** things to do now (change passwords, contact bank, warn contacts) â€” three, per CERC's rule of three; (5) a named support route.
- **The service-recovery corollary:** if something goes wrong (a lost report, a delayed response), an exceptional recovery can produce a *better* remembered experience than no problem at all â€” because the problem is the peak and the recovery is the end. Fund a real recovery path.
- **Duration neglect means you should optimize the peak and the end before you optimize form length.** A 12-minute form with a great ending beats an 8-minute form that ends in a bare "Submitted."

### 4.4 Serial position effect

Primacy (early items get more rehearsal) and recency (late items are still in working memory) produce the classic U-shaped recall curve; **the middle is the trough.** Von Restorff overrides position with distinctiveness (Â§1.2). â€” [IxDF](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/serial-position-effect-how-to-create-better-user-interfaces)

**CERC states this as an explicit crisis-communication rule:** "Spokespersons should state the most important messages first and last. In high-stress situations, listeners often do not hear or remember messages in the middle of a list." Paired with the **rule of three** â€” under stress, three messages, not seven. â€” [NOAA Practical Guide](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-08/Natural_Hazard_Risk_Communication_Practical_Guide.pdf)

**Design decisions:**
- **Every actionable list is exactly 3 items where possible, never more than 5.** Three-item lists have almost no trough.
- **Order by consequence, not chronology or convenience:** highest-stakes action first, second-highest last, lowest in the middle. For post-report actions: *1. Call your bank. 2. Change the password on your email. 3. Turn on two-step verification.* â€” with the middle slot given to the item that is either self-evident or least costly to miss.
- **Apply CERC's Triple-T structure** to any page with more than one key message: state the three key messages in summary â†’ give supporting detail â†’ restate the three key messages. On a web page this maps to: a "What to do" summary box at the top, detailed sections, and a closing recap. This is deliberate, useful redundancy at the *page* level, distinct from the redundancy effect in Â§2.1 (which concerns duplicating the same content in two simultaneous channels).
- **In navigation and option lists, put the most-used and the most-critical at the ends.**
- **Use von Restorff to rescue anything stranded in the middle** â€” but only once per view.

### 4.5 Response-time thresholds and progress feedback

Nielsen's three limits, unchanged since 1993 and derived from human perception rather than hardware: â€” [NN/g, Response Time Limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/), [NN/g, Website Response Times](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/website-response-times/)

- **0.1 s (100 ms)** â€” feels instantaneous; the outcome feels caused by the user. No feedback needed beyond showing the result.
- **1.0 s** â€” the limit for uninterrupted flow of thought. The user notices the delay but retains a sense of control. Beyond 1s, indicate the system is working.
- **10 s** â€” the limit of attention. Past this, users task-switch; you need a **percent-done indicator** and a clearly signposted way to cancel, and you must assume the user will need to reorient on return.

**Doherty threshold (Doherty & Thadani, IBM 1982):** keeping system response under **~400 ms** produces disproportionate gains in throughput and engagement â€” "neither side waits on the other." It sits comfortably inside Nielsen's 1s flow window. â€” [UXGen summary](https://uxgenstudio.com/ux-laws/the-doherty-threshold/)

**Progress indicator guidance:** â€” [Jakob Nielsen, UX Roundup Aug 2026](https://jakobnielsenphd.substack.com/p/ux-roundup-20260810)

| Wait | Show |
|---|---|
| **< 1 s** | Nothing. "A spinner flashing for 0.3 seconds is visual noise that makes the system feel slower." |
| **1â€“2 s** | Immediate local state change (button pressed/disabled state, inline skeleton for the specific region) |
| **2â€“10 s** | Indeterminate indicator (spinner) â€” proves the system is alive |
| **> 10 s** | **Percent-done bar**, plus an honest time estimate if you can compute one, plus a cancel affordance |

Nielsen also cites Harrison et al. (CMU): a progress bar with **decelerating, backward-moving ribbing was perceived as 11% faster** than a plain bar of identical duration.

**Skeleton screens vs. spinners â€” be honest about the evidence.** Skeletons manage layout expectations and are widely adopted, but the research is mixed: some studies find perceived-speed improvements, others find skeletons perform *worse* than spinners or blank screens depending on context and familiarity. Nielsen classes skeletons with spinners as "indeterminate cousins" that "confirm activity but reveal nothing about the remaining amount."

**Design decisions:**
- Budget **â‰¤400 ms** for every primary interaction (field validation, step transition, button press acknowledgment). Return an optimistic UI state within **100â€“200 ms** even when the backend is slower.
- Use **skeletons only where the final layout is known and stable** â€” e.g., the "your previous reports" list. Do not skeleton a form step; just render it.
- **Ban "progress theater."** No bar that sprints to 99% and stalls, no bar that resets, no oscillating time estimate. On a site whose entire purpose is trust, a lying progress bar is a credibility failure (Fogg #10).
- **File upload** (evidence: screenshots, PDFs) is the one place >10s waits are likely. Show a real per-file percent-done bar, the filename, a cancel per file, and never block the rest of the form while uploading.
- **Never time out a form session silently.** If a session must expire, warn at 2 minutes remaining with an "I need more time" button (WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable), and preserve all entered data through the expiry. A stressed user who loses 20 minutes of painful narrative will not come back.

### 4.6 Labor illusion â€” and its ethical limits

Buell & Norton (*Management Science* 57(9), 2011): across five experiments, **operational transparency** â€” showing the work being done â€” increased perceived value, and participants sometimes **preferred sites with longer waits that displayed labor** to instant sites returning identical results. The mediating mechanism is perceived provider effort inducing **reciprocity**. â€” [Buell & Norton 2011](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376), [HBS PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf)

**When to show work on this site:**

- **After submission**, show the pipeline: "Received â†’ Assessed by a caseworker (within 5 working days) â†’ Matched against other reports â†’ Outcome." A visible pipeline is the honest, durable form of the labor illusion and directly serves Fogg #1 and #7.
- **In the case-status view**, show timestamped events. Operational transparency over time is more valuable than a clever loading animation.

**Ethical limit â€” state this explicitly to the team.** Buell & Norton found people *prefer longer waits with visible labor*. **Do not add artificial delay.** On a victim service, manufacturing a wait to seem more thorough is a deception, it burns the trust you are trying to build, and if discovered it confirms the user's worst fear about the site. Use the labor illusion only to *narrate work that is genuinely happening*.

## 5. Reading, Typography, and Legibility

### 5.1 Line length (measure)

Bringhurst's canonical range is **45â€“75 characters per line** for a single-column setting, with **66 CPL** the most-cited optimum. Tinker's work, as interpreted by Rayner & Pollatsek, points to an optimum around **52 CPL**; Dyson & Haselgrove found ~55 CPL supports effective reading at both normal and fast speeds; Ruder proposed 50â€“60. Saccade size is consistent in **number of characters**, not visual angle (Morrison & Rayner 1981), which is why CPL is the right metric rather than pixel width. â€” [Dyson, *Behaviour & Information Technology*](https://doi.org/10.1080/01449290410001715714), [UXPin](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/optimal-line-length-for-readability/)

Baymard found product descriptions wider than **80 CPL were skipped 41% more often** than those in the 60â€“70 CPL range.

**Design decisions:**
- **Target 55â€“65 CPL for body text; hard cap at 75.** Given a 17â€“18px body size, this lands the content column at roughly **`max-width: 34rem` (~544px) to 42rem (~672px)**. Use `max-width: 65ch` and let the browser compute it.
- Because these users are stressed and often older, **bias to the lower end (~55 CPL)**: shorter lines favor accuracy and careful reading, which is what a legal report demands.
- **Line-height 1.5** at these measures (WCAG 1.4.12 also requires that content survive a user-set line-height of 1.5). ODPHP recommends **130â€“150%** specifically to keep low-literacy readers from losing their place on line return. For measures approaching 75 CPL, raise to 1.6.
- **Paragraph spacing:** at least 0.75â€“1.0Ã— the body font-size between paragraphs (WCAG 1.4.12 requires tolerance of 2Ã— font-size). Never rely on first-line indent.

### 5.2 Font size and contrast

**Size.** ODPHP Health Literacy Online: minimum **16px / 12pt**; for older adults or people with vision problems, **19px / 14pt**. Systematic review of font size for older adults: older adults prefer larger sizes, and there is a **critical print size below which reading speed declines sharply** â€” but also a decline at extremely large sizes, so bigger is not monotonically better. **Recommended type scale for this portal:**

| Role | Size | Weight | Line-height |

**Contrast (WCAG 2.2):** â€” [W3C SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum), [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/Overview.html), [WebAIM](https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/)

- **Targets: 48Ã—48 CSS px for all primary controls**, exceeding both 2.5.8 and 2.5.5. Tremor and fine-motor degradation are documented consequences of acute stress.
- Note the base rate: **low contrast is the single most common accessibility failure**, appearing on ~81% of home pages in the WebAIM Million. Automate contrast checking in CI.

### 5.3 Readability, reading age, and information order

**Reading age.** GDS mandates plain English across GOV.UK and targets a **reading age of 9**. The rationale is not condescension: by age 9 a reader has ~5,000 common words that they recognize *by shape* rather than decoding letter-by-letter, and adults retain that advantage for the same words. GDS also notes that **higher-literacy readers prefer plain English**, and the preference for plain language *increases* with the complexity of the issue. â€” [GOV.UK content design guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk), [GDS clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)

CERC's independent target for crisis messaging is **6thâ€“8th grade** â€” compatible with reading age 9 to 13. Both converge on the same conclusion for a stressed audience.

**Hard rules, directly from GDS:**
- **Sentences under 25 words.** Split anything longer.
- **Paragraphs of no more than 5 sentences** â€” and on this site, aim for 1â€“3.
- **Active voice** ("We will send you a reference number," not "A reference number will be sent").
- **Short, common words**: *buy* not *purchase*, *help* not *assist*, *about* not *approximately*.
- **Avoid negative contractions** â€” "can't" and "don't" are frequently misread as their opposites. Write "cannot" and "do not." This matters enormously on a page giving safety instructions.
- **Define every acronym on first use**, or avoid it. "OTP," "APP fraud," "phishing," "SIM swap," "mule account" are all jargon to most victims.
- No idioms, no figures of speech, no ampersands standing in for "and."

**Inverted pyramid and front-loading.** Start with the conclusion; supporting detail and background follow. Front-load *every* level: the H1 is descriptive, the first sentence of the page is the main point, the first sentence of each paragraph is that paragraph's point, and the **first two or three words of each sentence carry information**. â€” [NN/g, Inverted Pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)

**Bolding as scan-anchors.** NN/g's classic study (Morkes & Nielsen) measured five writing-style variants of the same site:

| Variant | Usability gain vs. control |
|---|---|
| Scannable (bullets, bold keywords, captions, more headings, shorter sections) | **+47%** |
| Concise (~half the word count) | **+58%** |
| Objective (neutral rather than promotional) | **+27%** |
| **All three combined** | **+124%** |

A later application of the same guidelines to Sun's site produced a **+159%** measured usability improvement. â€” [NN/g, Concise, Scannable, and Objective](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/), [NN/g, Applying Writing Guidelines](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/applying-writing-guidelines-web-pages/)

**Design decisions:**
- **Bold only nouns and verbs that are scan-anchors** â€” the action, the actor, the amount, the deadline. Never bold a full sentence (that's just a heavier paragraph and it destroys the anchor function). Target roughly **one bold phrase per paragraph, maximum**.
- **The bold-only test:** read a page reading nothing but the bolded tokens. If the result is not a coherent, correct instruction, the bolding is wrong.
- **The heading-only test:** read nothing but H2s and H3s. If the result is not a correct summary, the headings are wrong. This is the layer-cake requirement from Â§1.3 made testable.
- Run Fleschâ€“Kincaid or Hemingway on every page; GDS suggests Hemingway for flagging long/complex sentences. Set a CI gate.
- **CERC's negative-dominance rule (1N = 3P):** in emotionally charged situations people weight negatives ~3Ã— positives, so **balance each negative message with three constructive ones**, and avoid absolutes ("no," "never," "nothing"). Practically: do not write "We cannot recover your money." Write "Most reports do not get money back directly. Reporting does three things: it lets us freeze accounts the scammers are still using, it links your case to others, and it gives you a reference your bank will accept."

### 5.4 Chunking: bullets vs. prose

- **Bullets for anything the user must *do* or *check*.** Parallel grammatical structure, each item starting with a verb, each item â‰¤ ~15 words, 3 items ideally and never more than 5â€“7. Bulleted lists are one of the specific mechanisms behind the +47% scannability result.
- **Prose for anything the user must *understand* or *feel*** â€” the empathy statement, the explanation of what happens to their report. Bulleting empathy makes it read as bureaucratic.
- **Numbered lists strictly for ordered sequences**, never for unordered sets. On mobile add ~44px tap spacing between list items that contain links (ODPHP).
- **Never nest more than one level.** A stressed user cannot hold a two-level hierarchy.
- **Tables only for genuine two-dimensional comparisons**, and they must reflow to stacked cards below ~640px.

### 5.5 ALL CAPS, italics, and justified text â€” the numbers

**ALL CAPS.** Tinker's *Legibility of Print* remains the reference: all-capital print **"greatly retards speed of reading."** Tinker (1955) measured **9.5â€“19.0% slower** for 5- and 10-minute limits and **13.9%** over a 20-minute period. Tinker & Paterson found **13.4%** in favor of lower case. A 1928 study measured all-caps at **11.8% slower â‰ˆ 38 words per minute**, and **nine-tenths of adult readers judged lower case more legible.** A 2019 study of elementary students found lowercase read **13% faster** (effect size 0.28 SD), replicating Tinker.

The mechanism: lowercase provides distinctive **word shapes** (ascenders, descenders) enabling whole-word recognition; all-caps forces letter-by-letter reading and occupies **â‰¥â…“ more space**, requiring more fixations for the same content. Tinker's conclusion: *"The use of all capitals should be dispensed with in every printing situation."* â€” [Tinker via Liquisearch](https://www.liquisearch.com/all_caps/readability), [Painting with Print, LWI](https://www.lwionline.org/sites/default/files/2016-09/25Painting.pdf), [ERIC EJ1204569](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1204569.pdf)

**Italics.** Slow reading by roughly **4.5%** over a five-minute span, and up to **10%** under poor lighting or small type. â€” [Painting with Print, LWI](https://www.lwionline.org/sites/default/files/2016-09/25Painting.pdf)

**Justified text.** The same research line reports a slowdown of approximately **8â€“11%**. Professionally typeset justification with hyphenation can perform on par with left-aligned text, but the word-processor/browser variety â€” which is what you get on the web â€” produces irregular inter-word spacing and "rivers" that disrupt saccades. Left alignment is recommended because it provides a consistent left return point.

**Design decisions:**
- **No ALL CAPS anywhere**, including button labels, eyebrow labels, table headers, and tab labels. Especially not on emergency instructions, where the 13% penalty compounds with an already 80%-reduced processing capacity. If you need emphasis, use weight (600/700) and size.
- **No italics for anything longer than 2â€“3 words.** Use them only for genuine conventional uses (a legal citation, a foreign term). Never italicize a whole warning, a whole caption, or placeholder-style helper text.
- **`text-align: left` everywhere; never `justify`.** Set `hyphens: none` for body copy (hyphenation increases decoding effort for low-literacy readers) and never center body text.
- **No underline except on links.** Conversely, **do underline all in-content links** â€” this is the primary non-color link indicator required by 1.4.1.
- **No letter-spacing on body text**, and no negative tracking on headings. WCAG 1.4.12 requires content to survive user-set letter-spacing of 0.12em and word-spacing of 0.16em.
- **No text over images**, no text shadows, no low-opacity text.

## 6. Color in a Safety / Emergency Context

### 6.1 Red â€” when it helps and when it hurts

Elliot, Maier et al. (*JEP: General* 2007, 4 experiments + 2 mechanism studies) proposed that red functions as a danger/failure cue in achievement contexts, evoking **avoidance motivation** and impairing performance, outside conscious awareness. A follow-up (Maier, Elliot & Lichtenfeld, *PSPB* 2008) identified **local-relative-to-global processing** â€” i.e., attentional narrowing â€” as the mediator. â€” [Elliot et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.136.1.154), [Maier et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167208323104)

**Be honest about the replication status:** large-scale replications and meta-analyses have largely failed to reproduce the red-impairs-performance effect, and the consensus now is that the color-priming effect on intellectual performance is **not robust**. Do not present it to stakeholders as settled.

**What *is* well-established and sufficient for design purposes:**
- Red is the near-universal conventional signal for danger/error/stop in Western interfaces and in safety signage, so it carries strong learned meaning.
- The proposed mediator â€” local-over-global attentional processing â€” is precisely the failure mode you are already fighting (Â§2.6). Even at low confidence in the effect, the *direction* of risk aligns with everything else about this audience.
- Red is the color scammers and phishing pages use to manufacture alarm ("URGENT: Your account will be suspended").

**Design decisions â€” a red budget:**

| Use red for | Do NOT use red for |
|---|---|
| **Inline field validation errors** (with icon + text) | Page backgrounds, headers, or full-bleed banners |
| **Genuinely destructive confirmations** ("Delete this draft") | The primary CTA â€” never a red "Report now" button |
| A small status dot/badge in a status table, always with a text label | General urgency, deadlines, or emphasis |
| | Headings, body text, or any large area of the page |

- **Cap red at ~5% of the pixels in any viewport.** Small, contained, semantic, and always accompanied by an icon and text.
- **Never use red as the *first* thing the user sees.** The landing page must be calm. A user arriving in panic and hitting a red alarm screen is pushed further right on the Yerkes-Dodson curve.
- **Never animate red.** No pulsing, no flashing (also WCAG 2.3.1: no more than three flashes per second).
- **No sirens, no warning-triangle-heavy iconography, no hazard stripes.** These are theatre and they read as either alarmist or fake.

### 6.2 Signaling urgency without inducing panic

Rank urgency signals by arousal cost, ascending, and use the cheapest one that works:

1. **Position** (top of page, first in reading order) â€” zero arousal cost. Use this first and most.
2. **Size** (a 32px phone number) â€” very low cost, extremely effective, survives greyscale.
3. **Whitespace isolation** (the urgent block sits alone with 48px+ of clear space) â€” very low cost.
4. **Weight** (600/700) â€” low cost.
5. **Enclosure** (a bordered common region with a 4px left accent bar) â€” low-moderate.
6. **Neutral-but-distinct fill** (a very light neutral or desaturated amber panel) â€” moderate.
7. **Saturated hue** â€” highest cost. Reserve.
8. **Motion** â€” banned.

**The core substitution:** replace *chromatic* urgency with *positional and typographic* urgency. A phone number set at 32px/700 at the very top of a calm page, surrounded by whitespace, is more noticeable *and* less arousing than the same number in 18px white-on-red.

**Language substitution:** replace imperative-plus-exclamation with **conditional-plus-consequence**. Not "ACT NOW!" but "If you sent money in the last 24 hours, call your bank first â€” they can sometimes stop the payment." The conditional gives the user an assessment they can actually perform; the consequence gives them the reason.

## 7. Motion and Animation

### 7.1 When motion helps vs. distracts

Motion is the **strongest preattentive attribute** (Â§1.1). That makes it powerful and dangerous: it captures attention involuntarily, so any motion in the periphery will pull a tunneled user away from the task.

**Motion helps** when it answers a spatial question the user would otherwise have to answer by reasoning:
- Where did this new element come from? (an inline error expanding directly below its field)
- Where did the thing I dismissed go?
- Is this panel the same object as the card I clicked, or a new one?
- Did my action register? (button press state)

**Motion distracts** when it is decorative, autonomous, or peripheral: parallax, scroll-triggered reveals, auto-advancing carousels, animated illustrations, floating chat bubbles, looping spinners on fast operations, animated number counters, ambient background motion.

**For this site, the whitelist is short:**
1. Button/input state feedback (â‰¤100ms)
2. Inline expand/collapse of a disclosure (â‰¤200ms)
3. Step transitions in the report flow (â‰¤250ms, and prefer a plain page load)
4. Determinate progress bar fill (linear, honest)
5. Focus ring appearance (instant, â‰¤50ms)

Everything else is off.

### 7.2 prefers-reduced-motion and vestibular safety

WCAG **2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (Level AAA)** requires that motion animation triggered by interaction be disableable unless essential. The stated rationale is vestibular disorder: reactions include **dizziness, nausea, and headaches**; parallax scrolling is called out by name. Related: **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide (A)** for any auto-moving content over 5 seconds, and **2.3.1 Three Flashes or Below Threshold (A)**. â€” [W3C Understanding 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions), [Deque University](https://dequeuniversity.com/resources/wcag2.1/2.3.3-animations-from-interactions)

Prevalence context: an estimated **~35% of adults over 40 have experienced some form of vestibular dysfunction.** â€” [Rocket Validator](https://rocketvalidator.com/glossary/reduced-motion-preference)

**Implementation â€” the canonical global reset:**

```css
@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce) {
  *,
  *::before,
  *::after {
    animation-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    animation-iteration-count: 1 !important;
    transition-duration: 0.01ms !important;
    scroll-behavior: auto !important;
  }
}
```

Two implementation notes that matter:
- Use **`0.01ms`, not `none`/`0`**, so that `animationend` and `transitionend` still fire and JavaScript that depends on them does not deadlock. `animation-iteration-count: 1` prevents infinite loops from replaying at near-zero duration.
- **`reduce` does not mean *no* motion.** A brief opacity fade under ~200ms is widely considered acceptable and preserves useful feedback. Do not over-correct into a fully static interface that strips out focus indicators or loading states â€” that removes information the movement was carrying. Prefer the pattern of wrapping *ambitious* motion in `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)` so it is opt-in by default. â€” [css-animation.com](https://www.css-animation.com/accessible-motion-architecture/prefers-reduced-motion-architecture/)

**Additionally, provide an in-page toggle.** WCAG 2.3.3 permits either an on-page control or honoring the OS preference. Most users do not know the OS setting exists, and a fraud victim on a borrowed or unfamiliar device certainly will not. A "Reduce motion" toggle in a persistent settings area, saved to a cookie, satisfies the criterion for everyone.

### 7.3 Duration guidance in milliseconds

| Hover / focus ring | **50ms** (or instant) | standard |

| Inline error appearing | **150ms** fade + height | decelerate |

| Modal/dialog enter | **200ms** | decelerate |

**Hard ceilings:** nothing above **300ms**; nothing that moves more than ~100px; nothing that scales more than 1.1Ã—; no rotation; no parallax; no scroll-jacking; `scroll-behavior: smooth` only outside `prefers-reduced-motion`.
