## 1. The real cybercrime.gov.in (NCRP)

### 1.1 What it is and who runs it

The **National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (NCRP)** at [cybercrime.gov.in](https://cybercrime.gov.in/) is operated by the **Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C)** under the **Ministry of Home Affairs**. Per [I4C's own page](http://i4c.mha.gov.in/ncrp.aspx), NCRP launched **30 August 2019** (replacing an older CCPWC-scheme portal that only accepted child sexual abuse material and rape/gang-rape content reports) and was "dedicated to the nation" by the Home Minister on **20 January 2020**. I4C was approved by Cabinet in October 2018 with an outlay of ~â‚¹415.86 crore, and was **elevated to an Attached Office of MHA in July 2024** ([The420.in](https://the420.in/india-cybercrime-data-i4c-lok-sabha-fraud-losses/)).

### 1.2 Actual UI and technology

The portal is a **classic ASP.NET WebForms application** â€” every URL is a `.aspx` page (`/Webform/Accept.aspx`, `/Webform/FAQ.aspx`, `/Webform/Crime_AuthoLogin.aspx`, `/Webform/cyber_suspect.aspx`). Concrete UI characteristics I could verify:

- **Homepage primary navigation** is three large category tiles/tabs, which is the *first* decision forced on the user:

### 1.3 The actual reporting flow (Other Cyber Crime), step by step

Reconstructed from the official [MHA Citizen Manual (v10)](https://nal.res.in/sites/default/files/inline-files/2MHA-CitizenManualReportOtherCyberCrime-v10%20%282%29.pdf) and corroborating guides:

### 1.6 1930, CFCFRMS, and the golden hour

- **1930** is the toll-free 24Ã—7 National Cyber Crime Helpline run by I4C. It **replaced the older 155260** (which ran only 09:00â€“18:00). Staffed in Hindi, English and major regional languages; free from any Indian mobile network.
- **CFCFRMS** (Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System) launched **2021**. It is the integration layer connecting **LEAs, banks, RBI, payment intermediaries, wallets and NPCI** â€” I4C cites **85 banks/payment intermediaries/wallets** onboarded. A 1930 call or an NCRP financial-fraud complaint raises a ticket that alerts the **beneficiary** bank to place a **temporary lien** on the recipient account.
- **The golden hour** is consistently defined by police sources as **the first 60 minutes, maximum 90â€“120 minutes**, after the debit â€” the window in which money is still sitting in the first intermediary wallet/account before being layered across mule accounts ([News18 interview with Bengaluru cyber police](https://www.news18.com/cities/bengaluru-news/the-golden-hour-in-cyber-crime-how-fast-must-you-report-to-recover-lost-money-skn-ws-l-9846794.html); [RTI Wiki 1930 script](https://righttoinformation.wiki/1930-helpline-cyber-fraud-script)). The temporary lien lasts **up to 7 working days**.
- **Formal complaint must be filed on NCRP within 24 hours** of the 1930 call to convert the ticket into a tracked complaint.
- **RBI limited-liability rule:** under RBI circular `DBR.No.Leg.BC.78/09.07.005/2017-18`, a customer reporting an unauthorised electronic transaction **within 3 working days has zero liability**, and the bank must credit back **within 10 working days**. Banks routinely resist this by citing "customer negligence" where an OTP was shared. âš ï¸ Verify current circular status before putting this in production copy.

**Design implication:** the golden hour is the entire product thesis. Any flow that puts registration, OTP, category taxonomy, ID upload and a 200-character essay *before* the act of freezing money is architecturally misaligned with the one mechanism that actually recovers funds. The correct first screen is *"Have you lost money in the last 2 hours? â†’ Call 1930 now"* as a `tel:` link, with everything else deferred.

**This is your template.** Lockup 3B / Header 3 is an officially sanctioned Government-of-India visual pattern that contains no emblem. **Header, fixed elements (DBIM Â§5.4):** engagement bar (search); user controls = **skip to main content, language selection, accessibility controls (A- / A / A+ and contrast)**; global navigation menu.

**Footer requirements (DBIM Â§5.6) â€” mandatory elements:**

### 3.3 Performance budget implications

Practical targets that follow from the above:

- Avoid webfont-blocking render; `font-display: swap`.

## 4. Language and typography

### 4.1 Devanagari on the web

**Structural facts** (from [Pooja Saxena, "Devanagari Type Anatomy", TypeTogether](https://www.type-together.com/devanagari-type-anatomy) and [Alphabettes, "Devanagari Typography 101"](https://www.alphabettes.org/devanagari-typography-101-a-guide-for-typesetting-with-latin/)):

- Devanagari letters **hang from a horizontal headline (à¤¶à¤¿à¤°à¥‹à¤°à¥‡à¤–à¤¾ / shirorekha)** as well as sitting on a baseline.
- **There is no x-height in Devanagari.** There is base-letter height (headline to baseline), plus **matras and marks extending both above and below**. Ukars and lower matras should be treated like Latin descenders when aligning baselines.
- Only one case. **No italics, no underlines** as reliable emphasis â€” underlining "does not work well in Devanagari" and italics "feel forced". Emphasis must come from **weight and scale**, or a break/stylisation of the shirorekha.
- Traditional diagonal stress axis at ~30â€“45Â°, opposite to Latin.
- Own punctuation: **danda `à¥¤` and double danda `à¥¥`**, though Latin punctuation is common.

**Vertical space â€” the concrete numbers:**

Google's Material guidance classifies Hindi/Devanagari as a **"Tall" script** requiring extra line height ([Material typography language support](https://www.mdui.org/en/design/1/style/typography.html)). Its two specific rules:

- **Line height: +0.1em over English-like languages, at every style.** Rationale given: Latin mostly occupies the lower portion of the em box below the x-height, whereas tall scripts have long ascenders/descenders and will clip between adjacent lines.
- **Font size: +1px over the English spec** for Title-through-Caption sizes; at Display sizes the English size is fine.

Noto Sans Devanagari metrics are cited as **x-height 0.48em, line height 1.3em** at regular weight. âš ï¸ This specific pair comes from a low-authority aggregator; treat 1.3em as a plausible floor rather than a verified spec.

**Practical recommendation:**

```css

  --lh-latin: 1.5;
  --lh-deva: 1.65;   /* â‰ˆ +0.15; comfortably clears matra + ukar stacking */
}

html[lang="en"] body { line-height: var(--lh-latin); }
html[lang="hi"] body { line-height: var(--lh-deva); font-size: 1.0625rem; } /* â‰ˆ +1px at 16px base */

/* Never clip Devanagari in single-line UI */
.btn, .chip, .tab, .input { line-height: 1.6; padding-block: .7em; overflow: visible; }
```

The most common real-world Devanagari bug is **matra clipping in fixed-height buttons, chips, tabs and single-line inputs**. Any component with a hard `height` and `overflow: hidden` will shear the top matra or the bottom ukar. Use padding-driven heights, never fixed heights, on anything that can hold Hindi.

**Font choices, with actual weights:**

- **Noto Sans Devanagari** â€” mandated by DBIM. Safest choice for "reads as official". Designed for metric harmony with Noto Sans Latin (shared weight distribution and spacing).
- **Mukta** â€” by **Ek Type** (Girish Dalvi & Yashodeep Gholap), **SIL OFL 1.1**, [GitHub](https://github.com/ektype/mukta), [Google Fonts](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Mukta). Seven weights (200â€“800). Humanist, mono-linear, **narrower glyphs so more content fits**, extensive conjuncts, hinted for screen. Siblings cover other scripts with a unified design: **Mukta Vaani** (Gujarati), **Mukta Mahee** (Gurmukhi), **Mukta Malar** (Tamil) â€” which matters if you ever expand past Hindi.
- **Hind** (Indian Type Foundry) â€” the other common open Devanagari UI face.

**Byte cost â€” this is the number people forget.** From [`@fontsource/mukta`](https://registry.npmjs.org/%40fontsource%2Fmukta):

|---|---|

### 4.2 Bilingual English/Hindi toggle â€” how to do it

Conventions and evidence:

- **DBIM places language selection in the header's fixed "user controls" cluster**, alongside "skip to main content" and accessibility controls. Put it there; that's where Indian users look.
- DBIM: *"Name in Hindi or other language should appear on selection of the language"* â€” the organisation name itself must switch, not just body content.
- **Label each language in its own script**, never in the other: `English` / `à¤¹à¤¿à¤¨à¥à¤¦à¥€`. Never `Hindi`. Never a flag. A globe icon alone tests poorly with low-literacy users; pair icon with text.
- **Persist the choice** (cookie + `localStorage`) and **honour `Accept-Language`** on first load, but always show the toggle â€” do not auto-switch silently.
- **Set `lang` correctly** on `<html>` and on any mixed-script inline spans. This is a WCAG 3.1.1/3.1.2 requirement, it's what makes Indic screen-reader pronunciation work, and the [IJRSML audit of Indian public health portals](https://ijrsml.org/multilingual-accessibility-in-indias-public-health-portals-a-usability-audit/) found missing `lang` tagging and Indic screen-reader failures to be recurring defects.
- **Content parity is the failure mode.** That same audit identifies "content parity gaps" and "inconsistent terminology" as the dominant multilingual problems on Indian government sites â€” the Hindi version is a stale, partial translation. For a victim-facing product, a partially-translated page is worse than an untranslated one, because the user cannot tell what they're missing. **Either translate a screen completely or mark it explicitly as English-only.**
- **Do not use machine translation silently on legal/procedural text.** If you use Bhashini, label it.
- Language choice must **survive the entire form flow** including validation errors â€” error messages in the wrong language are a classic Indian govtech bug.

### 4.3 Hinglish and code-mixing

The evidence is real but of mixed quality â€” be honest about that internally.

**Strong evidence:** a Microsoft Research mixed-methods study found that **multilingual users strongly prefer chatbots that code-mix**, with significantly higher ratings for naturalness and conversational ability versus monolingual bots, and identified an effective **"Nudge" policy** where the system introduces code-mixed cues and adapts based on user reciprocation (summarised in [arXiv 2512.01439](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01439), which also notes only ~10% of India's population understands English).

**Weaker evidence, directionally useful:** an industry playbook reports a Hinglish CTA (`à¤…à¤ªà¤¨à¤¾ SIP à¤¶à¥à¤°à¥‚ à¤•à¤°à¥‡à¤‚` / "Apna SIP Shuru Karein") **outperforming the English CTA by 18.4% in Tier-2/Tier-3 cities** ([ProductGrowth.in](https://productgrowth.in/resources/playbooks/growth/small-changes-ctr-lifts/)) â€” vendor case study, unaudited. **Reconciling these into a usable rule:** the distinction is **script-mixing vs. word-borrowing**.

- âœ… **Do** borrow English *words* inside Hindi sentences, written **in Devanagari**, where that is the word people actually use: `à¤“à¤Ÿà¥€à¤ªà¥€`, `à¤¬à¥ˆà¤‚à¤•`, `à¤¸à¥à¤•à¥à¤°à¥€à¤¨à¤¶à¥‰à¤Ÿ`, `à¤²à¤¿à¤‚à¤•`, `à¤¯à¥‚à¤ªà¥€à¤†à¤ˆ`, `à¤ªà¥à¤²à¤¿à¤¸`. ### 4.4 Numbers, currency, digit grouping

- **Indian grouping is 3-then-2s**, not 3-3-3. `1,00,000` = one lakh. `1,00,00,000` = one crore. ## 5. Trust and scam-adjacent design in India

### 5.1 What Indian scams actually look like â€” the do-not-resemble list

The dominant 2026 pattern is the **"digital arrest"** scam. Sources: [RTI Wiki "Real vs Fake Police Notice"](https://righttoinformation.wiki/real-vs-fake-police-notice-india), [PIB Fact Check coverage](https://www.digit.in/news/general/received-an-intelligence-bureau-letter-threatening-arrest-it-may-be-a-scam-how-to-stay-safe.html), [SBI advisory](https://sbi.bank.in/web/yono/blog/digital-arrest-scams-protect-yourself-from-cybercriminals), [TOI on CBI's ABHAY bot](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/delhi/meet-abhay-your-new-shield-against-digital-arrest/articleshow/131125102.cms).

**Visual and verbal signatures your portal must not share:**

1. **The fake State Emblem / agency crest at the top of a document.** Scammers put the Ashoka Lions, CBI, IB, Delhi Police, RBI and I4C crests on fabricated PDFs. A victim who has just been shown a fake emblem has been *conditioned to distrust emblems*. **This alone is a strong argument for the no-emblem constraint being a feature, not a limitation.**
2. **PDF-as-authority.** Scam notices are polished PDFs â€” letterhead, crest, scribbled signature, QR code â€” built from stock templates in Foxit/Nitro/Adobe and delivered over **WhatsApp or Telegram from a foreign or unknown number**. Avoid making a downloadable letterhead PDF the centrepiece of your output.
3. **WhatsApp as a government channel.** PIB Fact Check has stated flatly that government agencies **do not send legal notices via email, WhatsApp, Instagram or other messaging platforms**. Do not use WhatsApp green (`#25D366`), do not put a WhatsApp CTA in a position of authority, and do not imitate WhatsApp's chat-bubble UI.
4. **Red urgency banners, countdown timers, and blinking/marquee alerts.** "Your arrest warrant has been issued", "respond within 24 hours or face legal action". A red urgency banner is the scammer's core device. **Use amber/`#FFC107` and calm phrasing for time-sensitivity; reserve red strictly for validation errors.** Frame the golden hour as *help* ("Calling 1930 in the next hour gives the best chance of freezing the money"), never as *threat*.
5. **ALL-CAPS, especially all-caps Hindi and multiple exclamation marks.** All-caps Devanagari is not even a real thing typographically â€” it appears in scam material as caps-locked Latin transliteration or as heavy bold. DBIM independently forbids all-caps for sentences and paragraphs. **Never use `text-transform: uppercase` on any Hindi string.**
6. **Fabricated legal citations, mashed together.** Scam notices cite implausible combinations like *"IPC Â§420 + IT Act Â§66 + Aadhaar Act"*. If you cite law, cite it precisely and correctly (BNS 2023, BNSS 2023, IT Act 2000 with the right section) and **link to the actual text**. Sloppy legal citation reads as fraudulent to anyone who has just been shown a fake notice.
7. **Invented official-sounding nouns.** *"Digital arrest"*, *"digital house arrest"*, *"RBI Verification Escrow account"*, *"audit clearance"*, *"security deposit"*, *"case clearance fee"*. None of these exist. **Never coin an official-sounding term the user cannot verify elsewhere.** Every term you use should be findable on a `gov.in` site.
8. **Demands for secrecy and isolation** â€” "stay alone", "don't tell your family", "keep this confidential". Your product should do the loud opposite: **actively encourage the user to involve a family member**, given that 20.4% of digital-service households need outside help anyway.
9. **Requests to move to a video call, screen share, or install an app.** Never ask for any of these.
10. **Asking for money, OTP, PIN, CVV, full card number, or Aadhaar number.** Your portal should ask for **none of these, ever**, and should say so prominently and permanently. NCRP's mandatory National ID upload is, in this light, a genuine design hazard â€” it trains victims that "official portals ask you to upload your Aadhaar", which is precisely the behaviour scammers exploit.
11. **Lookalike domains.** The Cyber Blog India warning is explicit: *"There can be fake URLs with a similar user interface as the original website."* Scam clones of cybercrime.gov.in exist.

### 5.2 What makes Indian citizens trust a website

Evidence:

- **Visible security cues dominate abstract ones.** A 2025 Indian study found the strongest correlates of trust were **HTTPS indicators and padlock symbols, clear and accessible privacy policies, and sensitivity to insecure payment pages** â€” and explicitly concluded that *"consumers respond more strongly to easily visible and understandable security cues"* than to technical certifications like ISO 27001 ([DOI 10.2478/ssidt-2025-0003](https://doi.org/10.2478/ssidt-2025-0003)).

### 5.3 Signalling official-grade seriousness without the emblem

This is the design problem. The recommended strategy, in priority order:

**A. Adopt DBIM Lockup 3B / Header 3 structure wholesale.** It is the officially prescribed pattern for non-emblem entities. Reproduce its *architecture* â€” wordmark + tagline, search bar, `skip to main content` / language / `A- A A+` / contrast controls in the top-right, global nav, dark dense mandatory footer with policies, sitemap, help, feedback and a per-page "Last Updated On" â€” without any state asset. Structure carries most of the officialness signal; the emblem carries less than people assume.

**B. Adopt the DBIM palette and Noto Sans.** These are Bootstrap 5 semantic colours and an open Google font. Using them is not appropriation of anything, and it produces immediate visual kinship with `gov.in` properties.

**C. Substitute radical transparency for the domain signal.** You lack `gov.in`, which GIGW names as the primary authenticity cue. Compensate with a **persistent, prominent "Who we are and who we are not" disclosure** â€” not buried in an About page. State plainly: who runs this, that it is **not** a government body, that it has no endorsement from I4C/MHA, that it **cannot** register an FIR or freeze money, and **exactly what it does do**. Counterintuitively, explicit disavowal of authority increases trust with a scam-primed audience, because every scam *overclaims* authority. Being the only site that tells you what it *can't* do is a differentiator.

**D. Make the official destinations more prominent than your own brand.** The most trustworthy possible posture is to be visibly a **signpost to 1930 and cybercrime.gov.in**, not a competitor to them. Link out early, often, and with the real URLs spelled in full so users can verify them independently.

**E. Borrow institutional seriousness from typography and restraint, not decoration.**
- Left-aligned text, generous line height, no centred body copy.
- One accent colour, used sparingly. No gradients, no glassmorphism, no illustrations of smiling people, no stock photography of hooded hackers.
- Numbers set in tabular figures.
- No animation beyond focus/hover states. Motion reads as marketing.
- Plain, unhedged sentences. No exclamation marks anywhere, in any language.

**F. Do the verifiable-security things and show them.**
- HTTPS with HSTS; make the padlock the user's own browser affordance work for you.
- A short, plain-language, genuinely readable privacy policy linked from the header *and* footer, not just the footer.
- State the data retention period in a sentence.
- A permanent, visible line: **"We will never ask for your OTP, PIN, CVV, card number, Aadhaar number, or any payment."**
- Publish a "How to check this site is real" page, and give it a memorable short path.

**G. Colour: avoid the tricolour.** Saffron/white/green combined with any emblem-like mark will read as an endorsement claim and is exactly the register scam letterheads use. **DBIM's own palette is not tricolour** â€” its primary is Bootstrap blue `#0D6EFD` on white with a dark footer. Follow DBIM, not the flag. Blue is the safest primary: it is what NCRP, Sanchar Saathi, RBI CMS and most Indian government service portals already use, so it inherits the familiarity without inheriting the emblem.
